## 1. What exactly must a Geography chapter serve? Ten topics, subtopics, the 16 detailed specific examples, terminology

### Takeaway
The 2027–2029 syllabus has ten topics, five physical (Paper 1) and five human (Paper 2), with 16 "detailed specific
examples" (DSEs): 8 on Paper 1 and 8 on Paper 2. Tectonic hazards, rivers, coasts and four human topics each carry two
DSEs. "Case studies" became DSEs, MEDC/LEDC became HIC/MIC/LIC, climate change is new, and settlement, water and
weather have gone. I could confirm about half the DSE wordings from official extracts. The rest rest on the repo's
earlier transcription.

### Cited Findings

### Inferences
- Tectonic hazards is the only Paper 1 topic where both DSEs are events with a date, coordinates and a magnitude. That suits the KQ6 "dated scene" model better than any other topic.
- Because teachers choose the DSEs, a chapter should teach one main DSE in depth and give one or two "if your teacher chose X" alternatives. For tectonics, the CUP coursebook's Türkiye example makes the 2023 Kahramanmaraş earthquakes the safest main choice.
- The syllabus `syllabus` tags in kq6.js (FP1 to FP5, SC1 to SC4) map naturally onto 0460 codes (4.1.1 … 4.4.4), so the coverage lint can carry over unchanged in concept.

## 2. How are the 2027 papers built (Paper 1, Paper 2, Paper 4), and what resources and command words do they use?

### Takeaway
In the 0460/01 specimen, the compulsory Section A Question 1 of Paper 1 is a tectonic hazards question built on maps. It
uses a map of earthquakes in Alaska (plate direction, latitude and longitude, magnitudes and scales, then "describe the
distribution" for 4 marks) and a location map of Whakaari volcano. Questions 2 to 4 are coasts, ecosystems
(Antarctica and deforestation) and climate change, with no rivers question. In Paper 2, Section A is built around a
1:50 000 map of Kingston, Jamaica. Paper 4 has two 30-mark fieldwork questions (a beach pebble study and migration to
Rome).

### Inferences
- The specimen's compulsory tectonics question is the strongest exam argument for a tectonics chapter. Its opening items (plate direction, latitude and longitude, magnitude scales, distribution, the location of a volcano) are exactly what a globe teaches well. This is one specimen, though; a live paper's Section A could be on any Paper 1 topic.
- Resource types seen in the specimens are maps with a latitude and longitude grid (Alaska), location maps with places and bearings (Whakaari), labelled photographs or diagrams of landforms (Figure 2.1), world or continent maps (Figure 3.1), line graphs over time (CO₂ and temperature, 1960–2020), a 1:50 000 topographic map (Kingston) and data in an insert. A globe chapter can make the first, second, fourth and fifth of these. It cannot replace the 1:50 000 topographic map skill.
- Section A and Section B appear to follow different mark patterns. Q1 opens 1·1·2·2·4·3, while Q2–4 run 1·2·3·4·3·5·7. The existing practice sets copy only the Q2–4 pattern.

## 3. What do examiner reports say candidates do badly, and what could a map-based chapter fix?

### Takeaway
Reports from recent 0460 series repeat three problems. Case-study answers lack place-specific detail or fail to link
general factors to a specific place. Candidates misread the command word or the focus, for example writing about
volcanoes when asked about earthquakes. And they hold specific tectonic misconceptions, such as that viscous lava flows
faster, that earthquakes can be predicted well enough to evacuate everyone, that they can identify a convergent margin
but not explain it, and that they cite plate boundaries a map does not show. "Distribution" is also poorly understood.
All of these reports are from the pre-2027 syllabus (Paper 1 Geographical Themes), but the tectonic content carries
over.

### Cited Findings
- June 2023 report: the best case-study answers had "a clear focus on the question, developing or linking ideas and including place-specific information". "Where place-specific detail was also given, full marks were gained." Many weaker answers "knew what the factors of location were but could not convincingly link them to a specific location". It advises giving relevant place-specific information "without including detail which is not relevant to the question set", and credits naming an area or zone rather than a whole country ("Pipri rather than Pakistan") [official] — [0460 June 2023 examiner report](https://www.cambridgeinternational.org/Images/569587-june-2023-examiner-report.pdf)
- A 2023 report: "A common error was to misread the question and write about the advantages of living near volcanoes rather than where earthquakes occur". A misconception was that people live in earthquake-prone areas as "they can easily be predicted, and people can therefore fully evacuate before the earthquake". "Weaker responses tended to refer to plate boundaries despite no plates being shown on the map" [official; the summary did not say whether this was the June or November 2023 report] — [June 2023 report](https://www.cambridgeinternational.org/Images/569587-june-2023-examiner-report.pdf); [November 2023 report (papacambridge copy)](https://pastpapers.papacambridge.com/directories/CAIE/CAIE-pastpapers/upload/0460_w23_er.pdf)
- June 2024 report: "A common misconception was that viscous lava emanating from a stratovolcano travels faster than that from a shield volcano where the lava is runny and less viscous". "Many responses were overly long since too much time was spent on the height and steepness of the slope of the cone and distance away from settlements". Key messages are to follow the rubric, answer all parts and link explanations to why something happened [official] — [0460 June 2024 examiner report](https://www.cambridgeinternational.org/Images/569587-june-2024-examiner-report.pdf)
- March 2020 report: most candidates identified the convergent plate margin, but many "were often unable to explain the processes which would lead to volcanoes at this type of margin" [official copy on a third-party host] — [0460 March 2020 examiner report](https://aleveler.com/wp-content/uploads/past-papers/0460_m20_er.pdf)
- March 2025 Paper 1 report (third-party upload): common errors in understanding command words and giving relevant detail. On industrial location, many noted a site near the coast "but did not explain the significance". "Few answers showed understanding of the term 'distribution'" [secondary upload] — [Scribd: March 2025 report](https://www.scribd.com/document/936090016/Geography-march-2025-past-paper-exam-2025)
- Research on students outside the exam board finds the same patterns: many undergraduates do not link volcano location to plate boundaries, younger students think volcanoes are randomly scattered, and many tie eruptions to earthquakes as a cause — [InVEST Volcanic Concept Survey (ERIC)](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1164573.pdf); [Beyond Penguins: misconceptions](https://beyondpenguins.ehe.osu.edu/issue/earths-changing-surface/common-misconceptions-about-weathering-erosion-volcanoes-and-earthquakes)
- Commercial guides say high-scoring 2025 answers named the specific place, quoted statistics or dates and named it in the first sentence. Treat this as secondary; one papersdaddy page also contained filler misattributed to 2023 — [papersdaddy exam guide](https://www.papersdaddy.com/cambridge/igcse/geography-0460/exam-guide); [Tutopiya](https://www.tutopiya.com/blog/igcse/top-10-tested-topics/cambridge-igcse-geography-0460-top-10-tested-topics/)

### Inferences
Weaknesses a globe chapter can fix directly:
- **"Distribution"** (2025 report, specimen Q1(a)(v), 4 marks): a scene that shows 50 years of earthquakes and then models a full "describe the distribution" answer (pattern, named areas, latitude and longitude, anomaly) teaches the skill visually.
- **Place-specific detail** (2023 report, Table B Level 3): flying the camera from globe to region to district (for example Türkiye, then Kahramanmaraş, then Antakya) makes the "specific details about place" that Level 3 needs concrete. Each DSE scene should end with a "three place-specific details to memorise" card.
- **Process at margins** (2020 report): an animated cross-section in the convergent-boundary scene (subduction, then melting, then rising magma) answers "explain the processes".
- **Using only the evidence on the map** (2023 report): a skills scene where the plate layer is switched off and the reader must describe what the map shows.
- **Misconceptions as "Common mistake / In fact" cards:** viscous lava flows faster; earthquakes can be predicted and fully evacuated; earthquakes cause eruptions; volcanoes are random. Each one is documented above.
- **Command-word discipline:** the chapter's "Check yourself" prompts should rotate command words (describe, explain, evaluate) and show a one-line "what this word wants".

### Gaps
- I could not read the 2024–2025 reports question by question for coasts, tropical storms, rivers or climate change. Climate change is new, so there are no reports on it yet.
- No 2027-syllabus examiner report exists yet. The first will follow the March or June 2027 series.

## 4. Ranking the ten topics for a scroll-over-a-globe chapter

### Takeaway
Tectonic hazards ranks first by a clear margin. It was the compulsory specimen question, has two event DSEs, a truly
global pattern, documented misconceptions and an easy camera path. Climate change ranks second (new topic, global
data, a deep-time to 2020s journey, links to Antarctica and reefs). Coasts with tropical storms, coral reefs and
mangroves ranks third (two DSEs and storm tracks, but its landforms are local). Population and migration is the best
Paper 2 option.
