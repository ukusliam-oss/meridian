## 1. Structure of the Earth: thickness, depth, temperature, and is the mantle liquid? (scene 2)

### Takeaway
USGS's *This Dynamic Earth* gives round numbers a 15-year-old can use: oceanic crust about 5 km, continental crust
about 30 km on average (up to 100 km under big mountain ranges), mantle about 2,900 km, liquid outer core about
2,200 km, solid inner core about 1,250 km. The mantle is **not** liquid. It is solid rock that creeps very slowly. The
evidence is that S-waves, which cannot cross a liquid, pass through the mantle but not through the outer core.
Temperatures are less certain. A 2013 *Science* experiment puts the inner-core boundary at about 5,500 K (about
5,200 °C), so "about 5,000–6,000 °C" is a safe range for the core.

### Cited Findings
**Layer thicknesses (USGS, the recommended source for the scene)**
- Crust: "Beneath the oceans, the crust varies little in thickness, generally extending only to about 5 km. The thickness of the crust beneath continents is much more variable but averages about 30 km"; under large mountain ranges its base can reach 100 km [snippet] — [USGS, *This Dynamic Earth*, "Inside the Earth" (Kious & Tilling, 1996)](https://pubs.usgs.gov/gip/dynamic/inside.html); mirrored at [LibreTexts copy of *This Dynamic Earth*](https://geo.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Geology/The_Dynamic_Earth_(Kious_and_Tilling)/01%3A_Historical_Perspective/1.02%3A_Inside_the_Earth)
- Mantle: "Below the crust is the mantle, a dense, hot layer of semi-solid rock approximately 2,900 km thick" [snippet] — [USGS *This Dynamic Earth*, "Inside the Earth"](https://pubs.usgs.gov/gip/dynamic/inside.html)
- Core: "a 2,200 km-thick liquid outer core and a 1,250 km-thick solid inner core" [snippet] — same USGS page. A college course page gives the outer core as 2,250 km thick and the inner core radius as 1,250 km [snippet] — [Glendale Community College, Earth's Interior](https://gcc.glendale.edu/ppal/earth_interior.htm)
- Reference-model values (for a teacher's cross-check): mean Earth radius 6,371 km; core–mantle boundary at about 2,891 km depth; inner-core boundary at radius 1,221.5 km, which is about 5,150 km depth [training] — Dziewonski & Anderson (1981), "Preliminary reference Earth model", *Physics of the Earth and Planetary Interiors* 25, 297–356, doi:10.1016/0031-9201(81)90046-7. The USGS round numbers add up to 5 + 2,900 + 2,200 + 1,250 = 6,355 km, which is consistent.

**Lithosphere and asthenosphere**
- The Geological Society says that at a certain depth in the upper mantle, peridotite reaches 1,300 °C and gives rise to the asthenosphere, where rock is weaker than the mantle above and below. "The lithosphere moves as one over the weaker, plastic asthenosphere" [snippet] — [Geological Society, Plate Tectonics: Mechanical properties – lithosphere and asthenosphere](https://www.geolsoc.org.uk/Plate-Tectonics/Chap2-What-is-a-Plate/Mechanical-properties-lithosphere-and-asthenosphere)
- Lithosphere = crust plus the upper 80 to 100 km of the mantle; about 200 km thick under continents [snippet; university course page, internally inconsistent] — [Tel Aviv University course notes, lithosphere and asthenosphere](https://www.tau.ac.il/~morris/03411203/chapter3/litho-astheno-sphere.htm)
- Asthenosphere roughly 100–250 km below the surface [snippet; only a low-quality site gave a number, so do not quote it] — no reliable URL captured.
- Revision notes written for the 2027 syllabus split the mantle into asthenosphere and lower mantle and define the lithosphere as "the rigid upper part of the mantle together with the crust" [snippet] — [Save My Exams, Characteristics of the Earth's structure (0460, 2025 syllabus edition)](https://www.savemyexams.com/igcse/geography/cie/25/revision-notes/tectonic-hazards/earthquakes-and-volcanoes/characteristics-of-the-earths-structure)

**Crust types**
- Continental crust is about 2.7 g/cm³ and mostly granitic; oceanic crust is about 3.0 g/cm³ and basaltic (basalt and gabbro, richer in iron and magnesium). One university lecture gives granite 2.8 and basalt 2.9 [snippet] — [University of Miami lecture notes](https://www.beal-agulhas.earth.miami.edu/_assets/pdf/2_geography.pdf); [WorldAtlas, oceanic vs continental crust (secondary)](https://worldatlas.com/articles/are-there-differences-between-continental-crust-and-oceanic-crust.html); [LibreTexts lab, plate densities](https://geo.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Geology/GEOS%3A_A_Physical_Geology_Lab_Manual_for_California_Community_Colleges_(Branciforte_and_Haddad)/05%3A_Plate_Tectonics/5.04%3A_Activity_5C-_Plate_Densities)
- Oceanic crust is rarely older than about 180 million years (other sources say about 200 Ma) because it is recycled at subduction zones. The oldest continental rocks are about 4 billion years old [snippet; secondary] — [WorldAtlas](https://worldatlas.com/articles/are-there-differences-between-continental-crust-and-oceanic-crust.html)

**Temperatures**
- Anzellini et al., "Melting of Iron at Earth's Inner Core Boundary Based on Fast X-ray Diffraction", *Science* 340 (6131), 464–466, 26 April 2013, from ESRF beamline ID27. The ESRF highlight reports about 5,500 K near the inner-core boundary after allowing for a 700 ± 200 K melting-point lowering by light elements, "1000 degrees hotter than in a previous experiment run 20 years ago". Pressure at the solid–liquid core boundary is 3.3 million atmospheres [snippet] — [ESRF highlight 2013](https://www.esrf.fr/home/UsersAndScience/Publications/Highlights/highlights-2013/dec/dec1.html); [Universitat de València record of the paper](https://producciocientifica.uv.es/documentos/6416a6e15db420433b7b8c86)
- The paper's melting point of pure iron at that pressure is 6,230 ± 500 K (about 5,960 °C). That is where the popular "6,000 °C, as hot as the Sun's surface" comes from [training; the search summary did not show this number].
- Revision and reference sites give the inner core as 5,000–6,000 °C, the outer core as 4,000–6,000 °C and the upper mantle as 1,000–1,500 °C or 1,400–3,000 °C; the values differ by site [snippet; secondary] — [LibreTexts, Brief Overview of Earth's Structure](https://geo.libretexts.org/Courses/Gettysburg_College/Oceans_of_Data_(Gownaris)/02%3A_Plate_Tectonics_and_Ocean_Bathymetry/2.02%3A_A_Brief_Overview_of_Earth's_Structure); [Save My Exams O Level page](https://www.savemyexams.com/o-level/geography/cie/25/revision-notes/tectonic-hazards/earthquakes-and-volcanoes/characteristics-of-the-earths-structure/)
- The inner core is solid because of pressure, not because it is cooler [snippet; secondary] — [LibreTexts, Brief Overview of Earth's Structure](https://geo.libretexts.org/Courses/Gettysburg_College/Oceans_of_Data_(Gownaris)/02%3A_Plate_Tectonics_and_Ocean_Bathymetry/2.02%3A_A_Brief_Overview_of_Earth's_Structure)

**Is the mantle liquid?**
- S-waves are transverse and cannot pass through a liquid, so their passage through the mantle shows it is solid. A classroom role play: students shoulder to shoulder carry both wave types (solid); students who can slide past each other carry only P-waves (liquid) [snippet] — [Oak National Academy, KS4 Physics "Probing planet Earth"](https://thenational.academy/teachers/programmes/physics-secondary-ks4-higher-aqa/units/measuring-waves/lessons/probing-planet-earth)
- "The mantle is almost all solid rock. Less than 1% of it is liquid e.g. at spreading plate boundaries, subduction zones and hot-spots" [snippet; the summary did not say which of these two pages carries the sentence] — [NESTA misconceptions list](https://nestanet.org/misconceptions); [Geological Society sheet "Volcanoes and molten magma"](https://www.geolsoc.org.uk/~/media/shared/documents/education%20and%20careers/volcanoes%20moltenmagma.pdf?la=en)
- Earth Learning Idea notes that some textbooks avoid the concept by calling the mantle largely liquid, although the seismic evidence contradicts this. It uses putty to show one material can behave elastically, plastically and brittlely [snippet] — [Earth Learning Idea 78, "Bouncing, bending, breaking"](https://earthlearningidea.com/PDF/78_Bouncing_bending_breaking.pdf)
- IRIS: the idea of a "solid flowing" is discrepant for most students, so hands-on models of the asthenosphere (for example Potty Putty) help [snippet] — [IRIS classroom resource](https://clean.iris.edu/hq/inclass/downloads/optional/1275)
- The confusion is partly blamed on mixing up chemical layers (crust, mantle, core) with mechanical layers (lithosphere, asthenosphere) [snippet] — [Geological Society, mechanical properties page](https://www.geolsoc.org.uk/Plate-Tectonics/Chap2-What-is-a-Plate/Mechanical-properties-lithosphere-and-asthenosphere)
- A USGS-sourced FAQ: the mantle is mostly solid from the liquid outer core to the crust, but it can creep over the long term [snippet] — [Earth Observatory of Singapore FAQ, "Is the Earth's mantle made of liquid magma?"](https://www.earthobservatory.sg/faq-on-earth-sciences/earth’s-mantle-made-liquid-magma)

### Inferences
- Use the USGS numbers on the cross-section inset and label them "about". Suggested labels: crust 5 km (ocean) to 30 km (continent, up to 100 km under mountains); mantle down to about 2,900 km; outer core (liquid iron and nickel) 2,900–5,100 km; inner core (solid) 5,100–6,371 km; core about 5,000–6,000 °C.
- USGS's "semi-solid" is itself a simplification. For students: "The mantle is solid rock. It is hot, but the pressure keeps it solid. Over millions of years it creeps, a few centimetres a year, like very stiff putty. Magma forms only in small pockets, mostly at plate boundaries and hotspots." Avoid "plates float on molten rock". The creep rate is my inference from plate speeds, not a sourced mantle-flow rate.
- Card for scene 2. **Common mistake:** "The mantle is a sea of liquid magma." **In fact:** S-waves travel through the mantle, and they cannot travel through liquids, so the mantle is solid. Under 1% of it is melted (Oak National Academy; NESTA/Geological Society). The **outer core** is the liquid layer, which we know because S-waves do not cross it.
- Lithosphere versus crust is an exam trap: plates are lithosphere (crust plus rigid top of the mantle, roughly 100 km thick), not crust alone.

### Gaps
- No BGS or NASA page with layer numbers turned up; USGS is the only government source captured.
- The asthenosphere depth range has no reliable source here. Do not publish 100–250 km or 100–350 km until checked.
- Anzellini et al.'s 6,230 ± 500 K and the PREM depths are [training]; open the paper or PREM before quoting them.
- Verify that the 100 km "base of the crust under large mountain ranges" wording is on the USGS page itself (the snippet paraphrased it).

## 2. The major plates: names, oceanic or continental, rates, driving forces and syllabus wording (scene 3)

### Takeaway
In Bird's PB2002 model (52 plates), seven plates cover 81% of Earth: Pacific (20.5%), Africa, Antarctica, North America,
Eurasia, Australia and South America. The Pacific, Nazca and Cocos plates are almost entirely ocean. Most big plates
carry both continent and ocean, so a "continental plate" is really a plate with a continent on it. Relative speeds
range from under 2 cm/yr (Eurasia–North America across Iceland) to about 15 cm/yr on the East Pacific Rise and 26 cm/yr
at the northern Tonga Trench. Research names slab pull as the strongest driving force. Revision guides for the 2027
syllabus still lead with mantle convection, and some add slab pull.

### Cited Findings
**Plate models**
- PB2002: Bird, P. (2003), "An updated digital model of plate boundaries", *Geochemistry Geophysics Geosystems* 4(3), 1027, doi:10.1029/2001GC000252. GIS conversion by Hugo Ahlenius (Nordpil), licence ODC-By 1.0 — [fraxen/tectonicplates README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/fraxen/tectonicplates/master/README.md); [prior notes: datasets_and_licences.md]
- MORVEL: DeMets, Gordon & Argus (2010), "Geologically current plate motions", *Geophysical Journal International* 181, 1–80. Angular velocities for 25 plates that "collectively occupy 97 per cent of Earth's surface". Spreading rates are averaged over 0.78 Myr for fast and intermediate ridges and since 3.16 Ma for slow ridges. MORVEL splits the old Australia plate into Capricorn, Australia and Macquarie [snippet] — [MORVEL paper PDF (UCSD mirror)](https://topex.ucsd.edu/geodynamics/morvel.pdf); [University of Wisconsin news release](https://news.wisc.edu/twenty-year-study-yields-precise-model-of-tectonic-plate-movements)
- PB2002's Euler poles (Pacific held fixed) take the major plates from DeMets et al. [1994], i.e. NUVEL-1A: AF, AN, AR, AU, CO, EU, IN, NA, NZ and SA. Others come from later papers, e.g. Anatolia and Aegean from McClusky et al. [2000], Okhotsk from Cook et al. [1986], Mariana from Bird's own estimate [computed: read from the file] — [PB2002_poles.dat.txt](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/fraxen/tectonicplates/master/original/PB2002_poles.dat.txt)

**Method for the [computed] numbers (teacher-checkable)**
- Areas: spherical polygon area of each outline in [PB2002_plates.dig.txt](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/fraxen/tectonicplates/master/original/PB2002_plates.dig.txt), R = 6,371 km. The 52 areas sum to 510.1 million km², exactly Earth's surface, which checks the method.
- Land share: 20,000 evenly spaced (Fibonacci) points tested against PB2002 plates and Natural Earth 1:50m land (the `world-atlas` land-50m file already in the repo's `node_modules`). Global land came out at 28.8%, close to the true value of about 29%.
- Velocities: (a) the `velocity`, `divergence` and `right-lateral` columns of the nearest boundary step in [PB2002_steps.dat.txt](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/fraxen/tectonicplates/master/original/PB2002_steps.dat.txt); (b) v = (ω_A − ω_B) × r from the poles file. The two methods agree to within 0.5 mm/yr wherever both were used.

**What moves plates**
- Forsyth & Uyeda (1975) used inverse methods and found that, of the forces likely to drive plates, slab pull was the strongest. By the 1990s, calculations put slab pull an order of magnitude above ridge push, and by 1996 slab pull was "generally considered the dominant mechanism" [snippet; Wikipedia summary] — [Wikipedia, Slab pull](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slab_pull); [Wikipedia, Ridge push](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ridge_push)
- A 2006 JpGU abstract summarises Forsyth & Uyeda differently: trench length was the most important factor for plate speed among ridge, trench and transform lengths. Trench length stands in for slab pull [snippet] — [JpGU 2006 abstract J161-032](https://www2.jpgu.org/meeting/2006/pdf/J161/J161-032_e.pdf)
- Conrad & Lithgow-Bertelloni (2002), "How Mantle Slabs Drive Plate Tectonics", *Science*, doi:10.1126/science.1074161: the best fit to observed plate motions came from a model that combined slab pull with "slab suction" (the mantle flow that sinking slabs set up). The effect matters for slabs in the top 600–700 km of the mantle [snippet] — [University of Michigan news release](https://news.umich.edu/?p=4725)
- Schellart (2004) laboratory models: net slab pull can be up to twice the ridge-push force [snippet] — [Schellart 2004, *GRL* (course copy)](https://www.eps.mcgill.ca/~courses/c350/ProbSets/Schellart_2004_GRL.pdf)
- "Considerable debate remains about the driving forces of the tectonic plates and their relative contribution" [snippet] — [Wikipedia, Slab pull](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slab_pull)

### Inferences
- Scene 3 plate list: name the seven big plates plus Nazca, Cocos, Philippine Sea, Arabian, Indian (or Indo-Australian) and Juan de Fuca. Tag each as "mostly ocean" or "continent + ocean" using the land shares above, so the scene does not teach that "the Eurasian plate is continental crust".
- Suggested "how plates move" text, faithful to the research and still exam-safe: "Heat from the core keeps the solid mantle slowly moving (convection). Where an old, cold, dense oceanic plate sinks into the mantle at a trench, its weight pulls the rest of the plate after it (**slab pull**, the biggest force). At ocean ridges, the raised, hot plate slides away under gravity (**ridge push**, smaller)." An answer that says only "convection currents" is what textbooks teach and is probably credited. Whether Cambridge's mark schemes credit slab pull is not confirmed.
- Speed anchors for a 15-year-old: Iceland about 2 cm/yr; Atlantic about 2–3.5 cm/yr; San Andreas plates about 5 cm/yr; Nazca under South America about 7–8 cm/yr; Pacific at the Japan Trench about 8 cm/yr; the fastest, Tonga, about 25 cm/yr. Use one model for every chevron (PB2002/NUVEL-1A, since PB2002 is the layer the globe draws) and say so in the source line.
- NUVEL-1A (inside PB2002) is a few million years' average and runs faster than GPS for India–Eurasia and Nazca–South America. For text, use the published USGS/GPS numbers. For chevron lengths, PB2002 is consistent.

## 3. The boundary types, with named places, numbers, landforms and hazards (scenes 5–8)

### Takeaway
Every boundary type has a checkable number. Mid-Atlantic Ridge 2–3.5 cm/yr; Iceland 1.9 cm/yr in total, but only a few
mm/yr in the Þingvellir graben. Nazca under South America 6.5–8 cm/yr. Pacific at the Japan Trench 8.3 cm/yr. Mariana's
Challenger Deep 10,935 ± 6 m. India–Eurasia about 4–5 cm/yr, with the Himalaya rising about 1 cm/yr (USGS) and Everest
8,848.86 m. Pacific–North America about 4.8 cm/yr in total, of which the San Andreas strand takes 2–3.5 cm/yr. North
Anatolian Fault about 2.4 cm/yr; East Anatolian Fault about 1 cm/yr. Popular figures for Þingvellir (2 cm/yr) and for
Himalayan uplift (5–10 cm/yr) are wrong or mislabelled.

### Inferences
- Scene 5. Body: "Iceland sits on the Mid-Atlantic Ridge. Eurasia and North America separate by about 1.9 cm a year (MORVEL; PB2002 gives the same). At Þingvellir the ground between the plates sinks into a rift valley." **Common mistake:** "The rift at Þingvellir widens 2 cm every year." **In fact:** 2 cm/yr is the whole plate separation across Iceland, spread over zones tens of kilometres wide. The Þingvellir graben itself opens only a few millimetres a year, often in sudden jumps.
- Scene 6. Quote USGS's "about 65–80 mm/yr". For students: "about 7 cm a year, roughly the width of your palm every year." That analogy is mine, not sourced. Exam version: "Denser oceanic plate (about 3.0 g/cm³) sinks under lighter continental plate (about 2.7 g/cm³) → trench → the sinking plate releases water and the rock above it melts → magma rises → explosive volcanoes; friction where the plates lock → earthquakes." The water-induced melting step needs a source before publishing (see Gaps).
- Scene 7. Use "India still moves north-north-east into Asia at about 4–5 cm a year; the Himalaya rise about 1 cm a year (USGS)". Everest 8,848.86 m. No volcanoes, because continental crust is too light to sink into the mantle (the reasoning is standard but needs a cited sentence).
- Scene 8. Pacific–North America is about 4.8 cm/yr in total; the San Andreas strand takes about 2–3.5 cm/yr, and other faults take the rest. Türkiye bridge: NAF about 2.4 cm/yr, EAF about 1 cm/yr, both strike-slip, no volcanoes.
- One-model rule: if the globe's chevrons use PB2002, write "model: Bird 2003 / NUVEL-1A" in the facts list. The text can still quote USGS/GPS numbers with their own sources.

## 4. Global distribution: the Ring of Fire figures, hotspots, intraplate quakes and the full-mark "describe the distribution" answer (scenes 1 and 4)

### Takeaway
The "81%" is USGS's figure for the share of the **largest** earthquakes on the circum-Pacific belt, from USGS's
largest-earthquakes FAQ. "About 90%" of **all** earthquakes is on a separate USGS Ring of Fire graphic page. "75% of
active volcanoes" (and "452 volcanoes") comes from National Geographic Education, not USGS. Retellings that say
"active and dormant" misquote it. USGS counts about 1,350 potentially active volcanoes; GVP lists 1,222 with Holocene
eruptions. A full-mark answer gives the pattern, names places, adds coordinates or data from the map, and names an
exception. It describes and does not explain: the 2023 report penalised citing plate boundaries the map did not show.

## 5. Alaska–Aleutians: plate directions, convergence rate, the 1964 earthquake and other events (scene 9)

### Takeaway
The Pacific plate moves north-north-west to north-west relative to North America and sinks under it along the
Alaska–Aleutian megathrust. USGS gives about 5.5–7.5 cm/yr, rising westward, and PB2002 gives 53 mm/yr toward 345° at
Prince William Sound to 73 mm/yr toward 315° at Attu. Subduction becomes more oblique toward the west. The 27 March
1964 M9.2 earthquake (about 25 km deep, Prince William Sound) killed 131 people, 122 of them by tsunami. Later great
events (1965 M8.7 Rat Islands, 2020 M7.8 Simeonof, 2021 M8.2 Chignik, and the 2025 M8.8 Kamchatka event farther west)
give enough dated, located points for a specimen-style map.

### Cited Findings
**Plate setting and rates**
- USGS (2010): Pacific motion relative to North America is about 5.5–7.5 cm/yr, increasing from east to west. The Pacific plate moves north-west; the eastern arc is nearly head-on to the trench and the western arc nearly parallel, so "the subduction becomes more oblique as one travels west" [snippet] — [USGS Open-File Report 2010-1083-B, Seismicity of the Earth 1900–2010, Aleutian Arc](https://pubs.usgs.gov/of/2010/1083/b/)
- A 2003 USGS poster: 6.6 cm/yr in the east to 8.6 cm/yr near the western edge [snippet] — [USGS poster, 17 November 2003 Rat Islands earthquake](https://earthquake.usgs.gov/product/poster/20031117/us/1457985121875/poster.pdf)
- IRIS: about 7 cm/yr is typical; 5.5 cm/yr in the Gulf of Alaska to 7.8 cm/yr at the western end [snippet] — [IRIS Teachable Moment, Alaska, 24 June 2011](https://web-archive-2022.iris.edu/hq/files/programs/education_and_outreach/retm/tm_110624_alaska/110624_alaska.pdf)
- Alaska DGGS: the Pacific plate dives beneath the North American plate along the Alaska–Aleutian Megathrust, part of the Ring of Fire, at "~5.5+ cm/yr (~2.2+ in/yr)". Alaska is the most seismically active US state. On average it has one M8+ every 13 years, one M7–8 every two years and six M6–7 a year (Alaska Seismic Hazard Safety Commission, 2012). The Fairweather fault is the main strike-slip structure in south-east Alaska [snippet] — [Alaska DGGS, Earthquakes and tsunamis](https://dggs.alaska.gov/popular-geology/earthquakes-tsunamis.html); [Alaska DGGS, Earthquakes](https://dggs.alaska.gov/hazards/earthquakes.html)
- A USGS fact sheet describes the Pacific plate as moving "steadily northward at a rate of about 2 inches per year", descending beneath the North American plate [snippet] — [USGS Fact Sheet 014-03, Alaska](https://pubs.usgs.gov/fs/old.2003/fs014-03/alaska.html)
- Secondary values: 6.3–7.4 cm/yr (Wikipedia, citing DeMets 1999) and 5.1–7.5 cm/yr [snippet] — [Wikipedia, Aleutian Arc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aleutian_Arc); [Wikipedia, Aleutian subduction zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aleutian_subduction_zone)
- PB2002 [computed], Pacific relative to North America: 1964 epicentre (60.9°N, 147.3°W) 53.0 mm/yr toward 345° (NNW); Kodiak (57.8°N, 152.4°W) 56.8 mm/yr toward 339° (NNW); Unalaska (53.9°N, 166.5°W) 65.4 mm/yr toward 327° (NW); Adak (51.9°N, 176.6°W) 70.5 mm/yr toward 320° (NW); Attu (52.9°N, 173.2°E) 73.4 mm/yr toward 315° (NW). On the nearest trench steps the convergent component falls from about 64–68 mm/yr (Unimak–Andreanof) to about 15 mm/yr near the Near Islands, while the sideways component grows to 73 mm/yr. That is the oblique subduction USGS describes.

**Trench and arc**
- "The 3,400-km-long Aleutian trench" [snippet] — [USGS Open-File Report 95-61-B](https://pubs.usgs.gov/of/1995/0061b/report.pdf). Other sources say about 3,540 km, or 3,800 km for the whole trench, with almost no volcanism in the westernmost 1,000 km [snippet] — [VolcanoCafe (blog)](https://www.volcanocafe.org/an-exploration-of-the-aleutian-arc/)
- Maximum depth: Britannica gives about 8,109 m near 51°N, 178°W, and 7,620 m "in some places". The often-quoted 7,679 m was not confirmed [snippet] — [Britannica, Aleutian Trench](https://www.britannica.com/print/article/13948)
- The arc has about 80 Quaternary stratovolcanoes and calderas, 44 of them historically active (USGS 1995). PBS counts 41 historically active since 1760; Wikipedia lists 40 active from Mount Spurr to Buldir Island [snippet] — [USGS OFR 95-61-B](https://pubs.usgs.gov/of/1995/0061b/report.pdf); [PBS, Harriman Expedition lecture](https://www.pbs.org/harriman/explog/lectures/crossen1.html); [Wikipedia, Aleutian Arc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aleutian_Arc)

## 6. Documented student misconceptions and Cambridge examiner comments, each paired with the correct statement (cards for scenes 1–9)

### Takeaway
The research base is real but mostly about undergraduates, and full texts could not be opened. Mills, Tomas and
Lewthwaite (2017) is the only secondary-school plate-tectonics study found. With 95 Australian junior secondary
students, it found misconceptions mostly about how landforms form at boundaries and about what causes subduction.
King's analyses show textbooks themselves carry errors on Earth's structure and plate tectonics. Libarkin's work shows
students put plates deep underground. Cambridge's own reports add exam-skill errors: explaining instead of describing,
citing boundaries the map does not show, and naming a convergent margin without explaining its volcanoes.

### Cited Findings
**Research literature**
