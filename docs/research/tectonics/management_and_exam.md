## 1. Why people live in areas at risk from tectonic hazards (scene 17)

### Takeaway
The syllabus point is 4.3.1, "reasons why people live in areas at risk from earthquakes and volcanic eruptions". The
usual reasons are well documented in revision sources: fertile soil, geothermal energy, minerals, tourism, family and
attachment, poverty or lack of choice, and low perceived risk. This round found **no** authoritative numbers for
Merapi's slope population, Vesuvius's red zone or Iceland's geothermal share before the search budget ran out. Scene
17 cannot carry those figures yet.

### Cited Findings
- 2027–2029 syllabus structure [snippet of official PDF]: 4.3 is "The impacts of tectonic hazards". 4.3.1 covers
  the reasons why people live in areas at risk from earthquakes and volcanic eruptions. 4.3.2 and 4.3.3 cover the
  impacts of earthquakes and of volcanic eruptions. "Primary and secondary" appears only at 4.4.1, which is about
  primary and secondary *responses* — [0460 syllabus 2027–2029](https://www.cambridgeinternational.org/Images/718150-2027-2029-syllabus.pdf)
- Revision notes for the 2027 syllabus [secondary] give these reasons for living in risky places:
  - people are unaware of the risk;
  - people believe the benefits outweigh the dangers;
  - people are "too poor to move away";
  - economic reasons: fertile soils, tourism, minerals and geothermal energy;
  - social reasons: staying near family;
  - people feel more confident "as technologies and prediction improve".
  — [Save My Exams: Impacts of tectonic hazards (CIE IGCSE, 2027 spec)](https://www.savemyexams.com/igcse/geography/cie/25/revision-notes/tectonic-hazards/impacts-of-earthquakes-and-volcanoes/impacts-of-tectonic-hazards/)

### Inferences
- Scene 17's "Common mistake / In fact" card can be fully sourced now:
  - Mistake: "people live there because earthquakes can be predicted, so everyone can evacuate" (2023 report).
  - In fact: the USGS quote "Neither the USGS nor any other scientists have ever predicted a major earthquake."
  - Contrast: some volcanic eruptions can be forecast and evacuated, as at Merapi 2010, where 279,702 people were
    evacuated by 8 November.
- Kinahrejo is a strong "attachment to place / low perceived risk" vignette, because residents expected the flows to
  take another valley. The IJOG paper should be read in full before the card is worded.
- The syllabus groups "why people live there" under 4.3 *impacts*, so scene 17 sits correctly at the start of Part 5.

## 2. Impacts and level of development: Haiti 2010, Chile 2010, Christchurch 2011, Tōhoku 2011, Nepal 2015 (scene 18)

### Takeaway
Authoritative figures now exist for all five events, but the death tolls depend heavily on who counted, and when.
Haiti ranges from about 46,000 to 316,000. Tōhoku is 15,901 (police, direct deaths only) or 19,787 (fire agency,
including about 3,800 disaster-related deaths). Chile ranges from 521 to 577. The comparison that matters for the
syllabus is a Haiti/Chile pair in the same year. Haiti's M7.0 killed tens or hundreds of thousands and cost about
120% of GDP. Chile's M8.8 (roughly 500 times the energy, by standard magnitude scaling, not source-checked) killed about 525, mostly in old buildings and the
tsunami. The site's existing table needs two corrections: Haiti's lower bound and Tōhoku's wording.

## 3. Hazard management with evaluation evidence (scenes 28–29)

### Takeaway
The syllabus asks for "an evaluation of the strategies and techniques used to manage the impacts of earthquakes and
volcanic eruptions: monitoring, prediction, protection, planning and technology" (4.4.2, secondary copy). There is
strong evidence for a balanced verdict. Earthquakes cannot be predicted (USGS), but early warning buys seconds.
JMA's first warning came 8.6 s after detection in 2011 and more than 15 s before strong shaking in Tōhoku, but it
underestimated Tokyo. Code-built buildings work: Chile 2010. Hard tsunami defences can fail and can also buy time:
Taro and Kamaishi were overtopped, while Fudai's 15.5 m floodgate saved the village. Drills reach millions, as the
2008 ShakeOut did with more than 5 million people.

### Cited Findings

**Syllabus wording**

  impacts of earthquakes and volcanic eruptions: monitoring, prediction, protection, planning and technology"

**Prediction versus forecasting**
- USGS: "No. Neither the USGS nor any other scientists have ever predicted a major earthquake. We do not know how,
  and we do not expect to know how any time in the foreseeable future."
  - "USGS scientists can only calculate the probability that a significant earthquake will occur (shown on our
    hazard mapping) in a specific area within a certain number of years."
  - "An earthquake prediction must define 3 elements: 1) the date and time, 2) the location, and 3) the magnitude."
  - "The USGS focuses its efforts on the long-term mitigation of earthquake hazards and by helping to improve the
    safety of structures."

  — [USGS FAQ: Can you predict earthquakes?](https://www.usgs.gov/faqs/can-you-predict-earthquakes)
- After the 2023 Türkiye–Syria earthquake, claims that it had been predicted went viral. A USGS seismologist called

  - Whakaari's 9 December 2019 eruption was a steam-driven (phreatic) blast that "caught the world by surprise"

### Inferences
- Scene 28's "prediction vs forecasting" card can now be sourced:
  - **prediction** of an earthquake must give date, place and magnitude, and none has ever been made (USGS);
  - **forecasting** is a probability over years (USGS hazard maps), or, for volcanoes, an alert level raised from
    monitoring;
  - **early warning** is neither: it detects an earthquake that has already started and races the shaking (8.6 s
    after detection; more than 15 s of warning in Tōhoku).
- Scene 29's "for / against / verdict" blocks, with evidence:
  - **Early warning.** For: more than 15 s across Tōhoku in 2011. Against: it underestimated Tokyo. In Puebla 2017,
    a nearby epicentre left about 12 s or less. It cannot warn about the tsunami's height. Verdict: it buys seconds
    for stopping trains and taking cover, not for evacuating.
  - **Building codes.** For: Chile 2010, where few modern buildings collapsed and only about eight people died in
    them. Against: older stock and soft soils still failed, codes had to be tightened, and one badly built building
    (CTV) caused 62% of Christchurch's deaths. Verdict: the most effective for lives, but only where codes are
    enforced and old buildings are retrofitted.
  - **Seawalls.** For: Fudai, with no deaths behind its 15.5 m gate. Against: Taro's 10 m walls and Kamaishi's
    record breakwater were overtopped and destroyed. Verdict: they reduce impacts and buy evacuation time but can
    give false confidence. This is a "limits of technology" example for the specimen-style (c) item.
  - **Drills.** For: cheap, and they reached more than 5 million people in one day (ShakeOut 2008). Against: the
    benefit is hard to measure. No outcome study was found this round.
- An LIC versus HIC point for the reserved evaluation mark: high-cost technology (gas cameras at about £30,000,
  seismic networks, 63 m breakwaters) is concentrated in HICs. Cheaper technology (smartphone gas sensors, community
  mapping) narrows the gap.
