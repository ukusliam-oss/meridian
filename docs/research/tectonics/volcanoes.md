## 1. Volcano types, lava viscosity, eruption styles, activity status and global counts

### Takeaway
Silica controls viscosity: basalt has the least silica and erupts hottest (about 1,100–1,200 °C), so it is the runniest and travels farthest. Rhyolite has the most silica and erupts coolest (about 700–900 °C), so it is stiff and traps gas. That is why shield volcanoes are broad and gentle and composite cones are steep and explosive. Examiners report students getting the speed comparison backwards. Parícutin (1943–1952) is the best-documented cinder cone for scene 13: it rose from a cornfield on 20 February 1943 and grew to 424 m. For counts, use the USGS figure of about 1,350 potentially active volcanoes, of which about 500 have erupted in historical time.

### Cited Findings
**Viscosity, silica and temperature**
- USGS Yellowstone Volcano Observatory: "The high silica content of rhyolite makes it viscous and prevents gases from escaping easily." Basalt "has lower silica content than rhyolite and, therefore, is less viscous", and under the right conditions it can flow for hundreds of kilometres [primary, snippet] — [USGS YVO, "The many types of fluids that flow in Yellowstone"](https://www.usgs.gov/observatories/yvo/news/many-types-fluids-flow-yellowstone)
- Composition and eruption temperature: basaltic lava is 45–52 % SiO₂ and 1,100–1,200 °C; rhyolitic is 69–75 %+ SiO₂ and 700–900 °C [secondary, snippet; the table says it draws on USGS and others] — [VolcanoDB, Types of lava](https://volcanodb.com/types-of-lava). The range runs from "~700 degrees C (rhyolite) to ~1200 degrees C (basalt)" [secondary, snippet] — [UC Museum of Paleontology, Berkeley](https://ucmp.berkeley.edu/education/calandscape/session2/volcanoes2.html)
- Viscosity rises by many orders of magnitude from basalt to rhyolite, from tens of pascal-seconds for hot basalt to more than a billion for cool rhyolite [secondary, snippet] — [IEEE TechNav: magma](https://technav.ieee.org/topic/magma/)
- Examiner report, June 2024 (0460): "A common misconception was that viscous lava emanating from a stratovolcano travels faster than that from a shield volcano where the lava is runny and less viscous" [primary; carried over from `docs/research/geography/syllabus_and_topic_fit.md` and not re-checked this round. Note that the URL there uses the same Images number, 569587, as the June 2023 report, so check that the link opens the 2024 report] — [0460 June 2024 examiner report](https://www.cambridgeinternational.org/Images/569587-june-2024-examiner-report.pdf)

**How fast lava moves** (this supports the "In fact" side of the scene 12 card)
- USGS: the leading edges of basalt flows "can travel as fast as 10 km/h (6 mph) on steep slopes but they typically advance less than 1 km/h on gentle slopes". When a basalt flow is confined in a channel or lava tube on a steep slope, the main body "can reach velocities >30 km/h" [primary, snippet] — [USGS Volcano Hazards Program, "Lava flows destroy everything in their path"](https://www.usgs.gov/index.php/programs/VHP/lava-flows-destroy-everything-their-path)
- An extreme case: the 1801 Hualālai flow moved at "at least 10 m s⁻¹ (more than 40 km h⁻¹)" [primary paper, snippet; 1995 JGR paper, title not captured] — reported in the same search as the USGS page above. Do not quote it without the paper.

**Cinder cone: Parícutin, Mexico (recommended for scene 13)**
- It began on 20 February 1943 in the cornfield of the farmer Dionisio Pulido, who saw the ground "swell upward and crack, forming a fissure" [secondary, snippet] — [Wikipedia: Parícutin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Par%C3%ADcutin); [SDSU How Volcanoes Work: Parícutin](https://volcanoes.sdsu.edu/Paricutin.html)
- The cone was 336 m tall after about one year and 424 m (1,391 ft) above the cornfield when the eruption ended in 1952. The last burst of activity was in January–February 1952 [secondary, snippet] — [Wikipedia: Parícutin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Par%C3%ADcutin); [Amusing Planet](https://amusingplanet.com/2014/08/paricutin-volcano-that-grew-out-of.html)
- The eruption lasted nine years (1943–1952) and left a 424 m cone and a lava field of about 25 km². The 1943–1945 stages built most of the cone, and lava reached its maximum distance of about 4 km from the vent. The four-stage division is from Luhr and Simkin (1993) [secondary geoheritage summary citing a primary book, snippet] — [IUGS Geoheritage: Parícutin Volcano](https://iugs-geoheritage.org/?p=10748)
- Early growth: about 30–50 m by midday on 21 February, according to a USGS reconstruction quoted by one source (a UNAM summary gives about 30 m in the first 24 hours). Lava buried the villages of San Juan Parangaricutiro and Parícutin. Residents left safely because the lava moved slowly and resettled at Nuevo San Juan. The towers, facade and altar of the 17th-century church still stand above the lava [secondary, snippet] — [IUGS Geoheritage](https://iugs-geoheritage.org/?p=10748); [SDSU](https://volcanoes.sdsu.edu/Paricutin.html)
- The figures disagree in places. One Wikipedia copy gives more than 233 km² damaged by stone, ash and lava, while others give the roughly 25 km² lava field. Summit elevations of about 2,800 m or 3,170 m are heights above sea level, not the height of the cone [secondary, snippet] — same sources as above

**Cinder cone: Sunset Crater, Arizona (backup)**
- The eruption is now dated to about AD 1085, with a likely range of about 1075–1090. The older tree-ring date of AD 1064–1065 (Smiley 1958) is disputed: a 2009 re-examination of 39 tree-ring samples (Elson et al.) questioned it, and pottery dates put the eruption between AD 1025 and 1150 [secondary, snippet] — [Wikipedia: Sunset Crater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunset_Crater); [Archaeology Southwest](https://www.archaeologysouthwest.org/?p=5974); [GSA 2005 abstract](https://gsa.confex.com/gsa/2005AM/webprogram/Paper93241.html); [NPS brochure, 1992](https://www.npshistory.com/publications/sucr/brochures/1992.pdf)

**Active, dormant and extinct**
- USGS YVO: volcanoes that "erupted within the Holocene, or that have the potential to erupt again, are generally treated as 'active'". Being active does not require an eruption in progress. The article suggests splitting the category into "actively erupting" (Kīlauea) and "potentially active" (Mount Hood). "Dormant" is shorthand for an active volcano that is not erupting now; Mount Hood is "an active volcano that is currently dormant". "Extinct" means a volcano that is not expected to erupt again [primary, snippet] — [USGS YVO, "Active, dormant, and extinct: clarifying confusing classifications"](https://usgs.gov/observatories/yvo/news/active-dormant-and-extinct-clarifying-confusing-classifications)
- The terms have no hard-and-fast definitions. Hawaiʻi's SOEST notes that "dormant" and "extinct" are imprecise because the distinction depends on how often a volcano erupts as well as on the time since its last eruption [primary/academic, snippet] — [SOEST, University of Hawaiʻi](https://www.soest.hawaii.edu/GG/HCV/haw_activity.html); [AVO: Mount Edgecumbe changes from "dormant" to "active"](https://avo.alaska.edu/news/view/mount-edgecumbe-volcanic-field-changes-from-dormant-to-active-what-does-that-mean)
- The 1996 USGS glossary dates the Holocene to the last 10,000 years; other sources use about 11,700 years [primary, snippet] — [USGS Open-File 96-24 glossary](https://pubs.usgs.gov/of/1996/0024/glossary.html)

**Global counts**
- USGS: "There are about 1,350 potentially active volcanoes worldwide, aside from the continuous belts of volcanoes on the ocean floor at spreading centers like the Mid-Atlantic Ridge". About 500 of them have erupted in historical time, and about 170 are in the USA and its territories [primary, snippet] — [USGS FAQ: How many active volcanoes are there on Earth?](https://www.usgs.gov/faqs/how-many-active-volcanoes-are-there-earth)
- One search summary said the Smithsonian GVP site, in late December 2025, listed 46 volcanoes with continuing eruptions and 1,222 volcanoes thought to have erupted in the Holocene. It also said that the GVP FAQ states no exact count is possible because totals depend on how "active" is defined. Both points need checking on the site [primary, snippet; I could not fetch the GVP page] — [Smithsonian GVP home](https://volcano.si.edu/index.cfm)
- Eruptions per year from GVP data: an analysis of 1945–2015 found about 35 new eruptions a year, ranging from about 25 to 50, with no trend. A later average was about 33 [secondary analysis of GVP data, snippet] — [Metabunk analysis](https://www.metabunk.org/goto/post?id=153138); [Discover, "Volcanic activity isn't increasing across the planet"](https://www.discovermagazine.com/volcanic-activity-isnt-increasing-across-the-planet-44448)
- A snapshot for 2023: the GVP listed 49 eruptions continuing in mid-August, 66 volcanoes had erupted in 2023 so far, and 16 of those eruptions were new [secondary, snippet] — [Discover, "Can you name all the volcanoes erupting right now?"](https://www.discovermagazine.com/can-you-name-all-the-volcanoes-erupting-right-now-45413)

### Inferences
- For scene 12 ("Common mistake / In fact"), use the June 2024 examiner quote as the mistake. For the "In fact" side use USGS: basalt is runny because it has less silica, and in a tube on a steep slope it can exceed 30 km/h. Viscous lava has more silica and erupts several hundred degrees cooler, so it moves slowly and builds steep cones. A safe line for the page is "runny basalt flows farther and faster; sticky, silica-rich lava piles up near the vent".

That story also supports the "lava flows rarely kill" point.
- For counts on the page, quote the USGS FAQ (about 1,350 potentially active; about 500 erupted in historical time). It is a single, stable, citable sentence. GVP counts change week to week, so quote them only with an "as of" date.

## 2. Volcanic hazards, with figures

### Takeaway
Pyroclastic flows are the main killer near a volcano. USGS gives temperatures of 200–700 °C and speeds from about 100 km/h up to about 720 km/h (450 mph). Lahars kill far from the vent: the small 1985 eruption of Nevado del Ruiz melted summit ice, and lahars reached Armero about two hours later and killed about 23,000 people there. Lava flows rarely kill because people can outrun them. Ash travels farthest. Less than 1 mm can disrupt an airport, and roofs can collapse under more than 100 mm (usually more than 300 mm), especially when the ash is wet. Volcanic CO₂ killed more than 1,700 people at Lake Nyos in 1986. Jökulhlaups are floods from eruptions under ice; the largest historic ones, from Katla, peaked at about 100,000 m³/s.

### Cited Findings
**Pyroclastic flows**
- USGS: the temperatures inside pyroclastic flows "are generally between 200°C and 700°C (390–1300°F)". A USGS volcano hazards fact sheet says flows can travel at velocities "as great as 450 miles an hour" (about 720 km/h), depending on volume, the solid-to-gas ratio, temperature and slope. A USGS Cascades Volcano Observatory page gives a typical speed of "over 60 miles per hour (100 kilometers/hour)" [primary, snippet; the summary did not make clear which of the two USGS pages below holds which figure] — [USGS VHP, "Pyroclastic flows move fast and destroy everything in their path"](https://www.usgs.gov/programs/vhp/pyroclastic-flows-move-fast-and-destroy-everything-their-path); [USGS GIP, Mount St Helens: pyroclastic flows](https://pubs.usgs.gov/gip/msh//pyroclastic.html); [USGS CVO page, archived copy](https://corpora.tika.apache.org/base/docs/govdocs1/678/678336.html)
- I did not find "700 km/h" as a USGS figure. Wikipedia gives a typical average of about 100 km/h and a maximum of 700 km/h [secondary, snippet] — [Wikipedia: Pyroclastic flow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyroclastic_flow)

**Lahars: Nevado del Ruiz and Armero, 13 November 1985**
- USGS: "a relatively small explosive eruption" on 13 November 1985 sent pyroclastic flows and surges across the ice-covered summit. "Pumice and meltwater produced by the hot pyroclastic flows and surges swept into gullies and channels" and became lahars. They reached Armero "approximately two hours after the eruption". About 23,000 people died there, "most of the town's inhabitants" [primary, snippet] — [USGS HVO Volcano Watch, "Lessons learned from the Armero, Colombia tragedy"](https://www.usgs.gov/center-news/volcano-watch-lessons-learned-armero-colombia-tragedy); [USGS image: memorial at Armero](https://www.usgs.gov/media/images/memorial-armero-colombia-those-who-died-1985-lahar-p)
- A USGS publication gives "more than 25,000 people were killed (23,000 in the town of Armero and 2,000 in the town of Chinchiná)". Earth magazine gives 20,000 in Armero alone [primary and secondary, snippet; the summary did not say which USGS publication holds the 25,000 figure] — [USGS, "Preventing volcanic catastrophe: the U.S. International Volcano Disaster Assistance Program"](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70162577); [USGS, "Keeping watch over Colombia's slumbering volcanoes"](https://www.usgs.gov/publications/keeping-watch-over-colombias-slumbering-volcanoes); [Earth magazine, Benchmarks: 13 November 1985](https://www.earthmagazine.org/article/benchmarks-november-13-1985-nevado-del-ruiz-eruption-triggers-deadly-lahars)

**Tephra and ash**
- USGS: "very thick ash deposits (>100 mm, more commonly >300 mm) may cause roof collapse, although this ash thickness is rare" [primary, snippet] — [USGS Volcanic Ash Impacts: buildings](https://volcanoes.usgs.gov/volcanic_ash/buildings.html)
- USGS fact sheet: a dry layer of ash 4 inches (about 10 cm) thick weighs 120–200 pounds per square yard, and wet ash can weigh twice as much [primary, snippet] — [USGS Fact Sheet 027-00](https://pubs.usgs.gov/fs/fs027-00/)
- USGS: "volcanic ash accumulations of less than 1mm (0.04 in) may be sufficient to temporarily affect airport operations". In flight, melted ash can build up in engines and may lead to engine failure [primary, snippet] — [USGS: volcanic ash and airports (PDF)](https://volcanoes.usgs.gov/vsc/file_mngr/file-247/Airport.pdf)
- Wet ash conducts electricity and can cause short circuits, especially in high-voltage circuits and transformers. Flat and wide-span roofs are more vulnerable than steep or short-span ones [secondary guidance, snippet] — [University of Washington, facilities managers' ash guide](https://mitigate.be.uw.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/37/2017/05/Facilities-Managers-v1.pdf)

**Volcanic gases**
- Lake Nyos, Cameroon, 21 August 1986: CO₂ from deep volcanic activity built up dissolved in the bottom water and was released all at once. Because CO₂ is denser than air, the cloud hugged the ground and flowed down valleys. More than 1,700 people died, mostly by asphyxiation [academic, snippet] — [Oregon State University, "Lake Nyos: silent but deadly"](https://volcano.oregonstate.edu/news/lake-nyos-silent-deadly); [Earth magazine, Benchmarks: African killer lakes](https://www.earthmagazine.org/article/benchmarks-august-15-1984-august-21-1986-african-killer-lakes-erupt/)
- In historic times, sulfur dioxide, carbon dioxide, carbonic acid and hydrogen sulfide have all caused deaths [academic compilation, snippet] — [Oregon State University, "Deadly gases"](https://volcano.oregonstate.edu/deadly-gases)

**Lava flows**
- USGS: lava flows "can destroy all structures in their paths, but they advance so slowly that they seldom endanger people" [primary, snippet] — [USGS: lava flow and lava dome hazards at Mount Hood](https://usgs.gov/volcanoes/mount-hood/science/lava-flow-and-lava-dome-hazards-mount-hood)

**Jökulhlaups (glacial outburst floods)**
- Icelandic Met Office: jökulhlaups are "water floods released from ice dammed marginal or subglacial lakes". A subglacial eruption can cause one. They last from a few hours to a few weeks, and the largest historic ones, from Katla eruptions, reached "on the order of 100.000 m³s⁻¹, comparable to the discharge of the Amazon river". A flood with similar proportions of water and solid material is also called a lahar [primary, snippet] — [Icelandic Met Office: Glacial outburst](https://en.vedur.is/volcanoes/volcanic-hazards/glacial-outburst/)

### Inferences
- Armero gives a strong "In fact" for the scene 15 card "VEI = deaths". USGS calls the 1985 eruption "relatively small", yet it killed about 23,000 people in one town. Deaths depend on where people live and on warnings, not only on eruption size. Ruiz 1985 is usually rated VEI 3, but that rating was not verified this round, so the card can rest on the USGS wording "relatively small".
- The scene 14 inset on Merapi's slopes can order hazards by distance. Pyroclastic flows reach up to about 15 km at Merapi (see Q6). Lahars travel down the river valleys for months afterwards (Kali Putih). Ash reaches farthest, for example Yogyakarta.
- Quote USGS speeds as a range ("over 100 km/h; the fastest up to about 700 km/h"). Quote the 450 mph figure only after checking which USGS page states it.

### Gaps
- No USGS page on volcanic gases (SO₂, CO₂, H₂S, fluorine) was found. The Nyos figures come from Oregon State University and Earth magazine.
- Not searched: tephra size classes (ash under 2 mm, lapilli, bombs over 64 mm) and USGS wording on volcanic bombs.
- Nevado del Ruiz VEI (expected 3) and the Smithsonian entry for it were not checked.

## 3. Benefits of living near volcanoes, and how many people are exposed

### Takeaway
About 800 million people live within 100 km of a volcano that could erupt, in 86 countries (Brown et al. 2015; Loughlin et al. 2015). Iceland is the clearest benefits case. Geothermal energy heated 89.7 % of Iceland's floor space in 2019 (Orkustofnun). It supplies roughly 30 % of the country's electricity, with hydro supplying about 70 %; the exact share depends on the year and source. Merapi's farmers stay for fertile soils, chilli and dairy farming, and sand mining.

## 4. The VEI

### Takeaway
The VEI was defined by Newhall and Self (1982, *Journal of Geophysical Research* 87, 1231–1238). It runs from 0 to 8 and is based mainly on the volume of tephra ejected, together with eruption-column height and observations. VEI 0 is under 10,000 m³. VEI 1 to 2 is a ×100 step to 1,000,000 m³, and each level above that is ×10. VEI 4 therefore starts at 0.1 km³, VEI 5 at 1 km³ and so on. Eyjafjallajökull 2010 and Merapi 2010 are both VEI 4. Hunga Tonga 2022 is VEI 5 in GVP-derived listings and in one peer-reviewed paper, but it is often given as "5–6". Pinatubo 1991 (6) and Tambora 1815 (7) were not verified this round.

### Cited Findings
- The original paper: Newhall, C.G. and Self, S. (1982), "The volcanic explosivity index (VEI): an estimate of explosive magnitude for historical volcanism", *Journal of Geophysical Research* 87 (C2), 1231–1238 [primary bibliographic record, snippet] — [Alaska Volcano Observatory reference 15931](https://avo.alaska.edu/explore/reference/15931); [NASA NTRS record](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19820055486)
- Thresholds: a non-explosive eruption (VEI 0) ejects less than 10,000 m³ of tephra. From VEI 1 to VEI 2 the lower bound rises by a factor of 100, from 10,000 to 1,000,000 m³. Each higher level is a factor of 10. The index uses ejecta volume, eruption-cloud height and qualitative observations [secondary, snippet] — [Wikipedia: Volcanic explosivity index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcanic_explosivity_index); [NPS: Volcanic Explosivity Index](https://www.nps.gov/subjects/volcanoes/volcanic-explosivity-index.htm); [USGS YVO: "The Volcanic Explosivity Index: a tool for comparing the sizes of explosive volcanic eruptions"](https://usgs.gov/observatories/yvo/news/volcanic-explosivity-index-a-tool-comparing-sizes-explosive-volcanic)
- Eyjafjallajökull 2010, VEI 4: more than 0.1 km³ of tephra in the first 72 hours, "was einem Volcanic Explosivity Index (VEI) von 4 entspricht" [primary conference abstract, snippet] — [DACH2010-206 (Copernicus)](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/DACH2010/DACH2010-206.pdf). Wikipedia rates the first (flank) phase VEI 1. It rates the second (summit) phase VEI 4, with about 250 million m³ of tephra and a plume of about 9 km [secondary, snippet] — [Wikipedia: 2010 eruptions of Eyjafjallajökull](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_eruptions_of_Eyjafjallaj%C3%B6kull). I could not see the VEI field on the GVP page — [GVP Eyjafjallajökull 372020](https://volcano.si.edu/volcano.cfm?vn=372020)
- Merapi 2010, VEI 4: "A paroxysmal VEI 4 magmatic eruption (with ash column to 17 km altitude) destroyed this dome" [primary, USGS publication, snippet] — [Pallister et al., "Merapi 2010 eruption—Chronology and extrusion rates monitored with satellite radar and used in eruption forecasting" (USGS record 70193590)](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70193590); [Keele/Twente record: "An overview of the large-magnitude (VEI 4) eruption of Merapi in 2010"](https://keele-repository.worktribe.com/output/427357/an-overview-of-the-large-magnitude-vei-4-eruption-of-merapi-in-2010)
- Hunga Tonga 2022, VEI 5 or 6: Wikipedia gives "VEI: 5–6". A peer-reviewed paper says "possibly equivalent to VEI 5". Listings derived from GVP data give 5 [secondary and primary, snippet] — [Wikipedia: 2022 Hunga Tonga–Hunga Haʻapai eruption and tsunami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2022_Hunga_Tonga%E2%80%93Hunga_Ha%CA%BBapai_eruption_and_tsunami); [Geoscience Letters, 2022](https://geoscienceletters.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s40562-022-00232-z); [GVP Hunga Tonga-Hunga Ha'apai 243040](https://volcano.si.edu/volcano.cfm?vn=243040)
- The volumes behind the disagreement over 5 or 6: more than 6.3 km³ of uncompacted pyroclastic material settled on the seafloor, and the caldera volume changed by 6 km³ dense-rock equivalent. At least 1.8 km³ of airfall is a minimum, estimated from ocean discoloration [primary, snippet] — [Kelly et al. 2024, *Bulletin of Volcanology*, record at Penn State](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/airfall-volume-of-the-15-january-2022-eruption-of-hunga-volcano-e/). A seafloor survey found the caldera floor had dropped from about 150 m to 850 m below sea level, and about 8.9 km³ in total had been displaced [secondary news report of a primary study, snippet] — [Ocean News: bathymetric surveys](https://oceannews.com/news/science-technology/bathymetric-surveys-reveal-hunga-volcanos-seafloor-transformation/)

### Inferences
- The levels follow from the cited rule (VEI 0 under 10⁴ m³; VEI 2 from 10⁶ m³; ×10 per step after that). VEI 1 is ≥10⁴ m³, VEI 2 ≥10⁶ m³ (0.001 km³), VEI 3 ≥10⁷ m³ (0.01 km³), VEI 4 ≥0.1 km³, VEI 5 ≥1 km³, VEI 6 ≥10 km³, VEI 7 ≥100 km³ and VEI 8 ≥1,000 km³. The DACH2010 abstract independently confirms the VEI 4 step at 0.1 km³. Check this ladder against the NPS or USGS table before publishing it.
- Hunga's erupted volume (roughly 6–9 km³ by the figures above) falls in the VEI 5 band, which is below 10 km³. Its 57 km plume is like a VEI 6 column. That is the likely reason sources split. For the scene 15 ladder, use **"VEI 5 (some scientists argue 6)"** and say why: by volume it is a 5, by plume height it looks like a 6. That also teaches that the VEI measures size, not deaths.
- The scene 15 "VEI = deaths" card: Hunga Tonga (VEI 5–6) killed about 6 people across the Pacific. The "relatively small" Ruiz eruption of 1985 killed about 23,000. Merapi 2010 (VEI 4) killed about 350–390.

### Gaps
- The VEI value on the Smithsonian GVP page could not be read for any of the five eruptions.
- Pinatubo 1991 and Tambora 1815 were not verified (the search budget ran out). Expected values, unverified: Pinatubo VEI 6 and Tambora VEI 7.
- The plume-height column of the Newhall and Self table (for example, over 25 km for VEI 5 and above) was not seen in any source.

## 5. Eyjafjallajökull, Iceland, 2010 (main DSE, syllabus 4.4.4)

### Takeaway
Iceland sits on the Mid-Atlantic Ridge. Eyjafjallajökull had 18 years of unrest before a basalt flank eruption at Fimmvörðuháls (20 March to 12 April 2010). The explosive summit eruption under the ice cap (14 April to about 22 May) produced unusually fine, fluorine-rich ash, because magma met meltwater and shattered. More than 800 people were evacuated before dawn on 14 April as jökulhlaups ran off the ice. Ash then closed much of Europe's airspace. Eurocontrol counts 104,000 flights cancelled from 15 to 22 April and about 10 million passenger journeys disrupted. IATA put airline losses at more than US$1.7 billion. The effects reached Kenya's flower farms. The response changed the rules: on 20 April a tolerance of 2 mg/m³ of ash was accepted, three ash-concentration zones followed from 17 May, and the EU and ICAO created new crisis bodies.

### Cited Findings
**Causes (setting, precursors, why the ash was fine)**
- Unrest: the 2010 events were "the culmination of 18-years of intermittent volcanic unrest". Intrusions in 1994 and 1999 were mapped with tilt, GPS and InSAR as sills at 4.5–6.5 km depth. From 2000 to 2009 earthquakes ran at 1–4 a month and the ground hardly moved [primary, snippet] — [Sigmundsson et al. 2010, "Intrusion triggering of the 2010 Eyjafjallajökull explosive eruption", *Nature* 468, 426–432](https://dx.doi.org/10.1038/nature09558); [NERC COMET volcano portal](https://comet.nerc.ac.uk/volcanoes/eyjafjallajokull)
- Late in summer 2009 a small shift at a GPS station led scientists to watch more closely. In early January 2010 deformation and earthquakes both increased. Extra GPS stations then showed rapid inflation as magma moved up [primary institution, snippet] — [University of Wisconsin news release](https://news.wisc.edu/18684). The flanks had risen about 15 cm by the start of the eruption [primary EU summary, snippet] — [CORDIS](https://cordis.europa.eu/article/id/32774-tick-tock-tick-tock-a-volcanos-about-to-erupt)
- Phase 1: a fissure eruption began on 20 March 2010 at Fimmvörðuháls on the east flank. Phase 2: the summit caldera erupted from 14 April [primary, snippet] — [GVP Eyjafjallajökull](https://volcano.si.edu/volcano.cfm?vn=372020). The flank eruption ran 20 March to 12 April and was small and effusive; the summit eruption ran 14 April to 23 May and was mostly explosive [primary slides, snippet] — [Thordarson et al. 2010 workshop slides](https://dots.el.erdc.dren.mil/workshops/2010-06-06-Climate/20-Thordarson_et_al_Eyjafjoll_eruption-Ranga.pdf)
- Composition: the Fimmvörðuháls lava was alkali-olivine basalt at about 47 % SiO₂. The summit tephra was more evolved, at about 58 % SiO₂, with high fluorine [academic database, snippet] — [Tephrabase: Eyjafjallajökull 2010](https://tephrabase.org/ey2010.html). The Icelandic Met Office described the ash as "fluorine rich, of intermediate silica content and the particles are very fine" [primary, snippet] — [Icelandic Met Office article](https://en.vedur.is/earthquakes-and-volcanism/articles/nr/1880)
- Why the ash was fine: the eruption melted the overlying ice cap, and magma meeting ice produced "a fine ash that could remain suspended for long periods" [primary, snippet] — [GVP Eyjafjallajökull](https://volcano.si.edu/volcano.cfm?vn=372020). The first 15–17 hours were phreatomagmatic, as the eruption melted its way through the ice-filled summit caldera [primary abstract, snippet] — [Höskuldsson et al., EGU2011-14165](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU2011/EGU2011-14165.pdf)
- Totals: about 4.8×10¹¹ kg of material, about 0.18 km³ dense-rock equivalent. About 80 % was airborne tephra, with a bulk volume of 0.27 km³, carried by plumes 3–10 km high. At least 7×10¹⁰ kg (70 Tg) was very fine ash under 28 µm. About 50 % fell in Iceland. The rest was detected over about 7 million km² of Europe and the North Atlantic, but less than 0.02 % (under 10⁸ kg) reached mainland Europe [primary, peer-reviewed, snippet] — [Gudmundsson et al. 2012, "Ash generation and distribution from the April–May 2010 eruption of Eyjafjallajökull, Iceland", *Scientific Reports* 2:572 (PMC3418519)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3418519); [NILU repository record](https://nilu.brage.unit.no/nilu-xmlui/handle/11250/2650992)
- Plume heights: on 14 April the London VAAC reported ash at 20,000 ft (6,100 m) or more, and by 2:25 pm local time the plume stretched more than 100 km from the vent [primary, snippet] — [NASA Earth Observatory, "Eruption of Eyjafjallajökull Volcano, Iceland"](https://earthobservatory.nasa.gov/images/43676/eruption-of-eyjafjallajokull-volcano-iceland). On 5–6 May ash production rose again and the plume reached 8–9 km [secondary book, snippet] — [timarit.is (Icelandic book on the eruption)](https://timarit.is/page/7011834?lang=en). Gudmundsson et al. give 3–10 km over the whole eruption (above)
- End: the last day of sustained activity was 22 May 2010. By 23 June the Icelandic Met Office (IMO) and the University of Iceland's Institute of Earth Sciences had stopped regular status reports. The eruption was reported over in late October 2010 [primary, snippet] — [GVP Eyjafjallajökull](https://volcano.si.edu/volcano.cfm?vn=372020)

**Impacts in Iceland**
- "Subglacial volcanism in the summit caldera led to a pre-dawn evacuation of over 800 inhabitants on April 14". Jökulhlaups drained down both the north and south flanks of the ice cap. A second flood on 15 April carried large amounts of glacial ice down the Markarfljót and forced another local evacuation. Repeated floods damaged infrastructure and covered farmland [primary, snippet; the summary credits this to the IMO, through one of the two pages below] — [Icelandic Met Office article](https://en.vedur.is/earthquakes-and-volcanism/articles/nr/1880); [Icelandic Met Office news](https://en.vedur.is/about-imo/news/nr/2251)
- Fluoride from ash that animals eat or breathe in can weaken bones and teeth after heavy exposure over several days. The eruption of 1821–23 also produced fluoride-rich ash, which farmers blamed for cattle and sheep deaths [secondary press, snippet] — [IPS News](https://www.ipsnews.net/?p=40537); [Newsdesk, "Icelandic animals are feeling the hurt"](https://newsdesk.org/2010/04/20/icelandic-animals-are-feeling-the-hurt/); [ISKCON News, "Iceland's farmers try to save herds from toxic ash"](https://iskconnews.org/icelands-farmers-try-to-save-herds-from-toxic-ash/)
- A dairy farmer, Berglind Hilmarsdóttir, gathered about 120 cattle, but some were lost in the ash fog. Many farmers went back to their animals despite the flood risk [secondary press, snippet; the summary did not say which of the three outlets above carried this] — same sources as above
- Tourism impact and recovery: see Q3 (the projected £180 million shortfall and "Inspired by Iceland").

**Impacts in Europe and beyond: the airspace closure**
- Eurocontrol: "The main period of the crisis was 15th–22nd April." "104,000 flights were cancelled during the 8-day crisis", which was about 48 % of expected traffic, peaking at 80 % on 18 April. The May eruptions caused about 7,000 more cancellations. More than 5,000 extra flights were run to reposition aircraft and crews and to bring stranded passengers home. Its summary rounds the totals to about 100,000 flights and 10 million passenger journeys [primary, snippet] — [Eurocontrol, "Ash-cloud of April and May 2010: Impact on Air Traffic"](https://www.eurocontrol.int/publication/ash-cloud-april-and-may-2010-impact-air-traffic); [Eurocontrol archive document](https://www.eurocontrol.int/archive_download/all/node/10542)
- Day by day: actual flights fell from 28,087 on 14 April to 4,886 on 17 April. By 18 April Eurocontrol expected more than 63,000 flights to have been cancelled since 15 April. Traffic was back to normal only on Friday 23 April 2010. National authorities decided the closures, and Eurocontrol handled communication and traffic flow [primary, snippet] — [Eurocontrol update via CAPA](https://centreforaviation.com/members/direct-news/eurocontrol-update-on-european-air-traffic-situation-25029); [Eurocontrol archive document](https://www.eurocontrol.int/archive_download/all/node/10542)
- USGS: "over 300 airports in about two dozen countries, and a correspondingly large airspace, were closed in Europe during 15–21 April 2010". Over 100,000 flights were cancelled that week, affecting 7 million passengers [primary, snippet] — [USGS: ash clouds and air routes, Eyjafjallajökull](https://volcanoes.usgs.gov/volcanic_ash/ash_clouds_air_routes_eyjafjallajokull.html). Closures hit the UK, Ireland, Denmark, Norway, Sweden, Finland and Belgium, with wide airport closures in France and Germany [secondary trade press, snippet] — [CAPA](https://centreforaviation.com/news/iceland-volcanic-eruption-closes-northern-european-airspace-45294). Wikipedia calls it the largest air-traffic shutdown since the Second World War [secondary] — [Wikipedia: Air travel disruption after the 2010 Eyjafjallajökull eruption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_travel_disruption_after_the_2010_Eyjafjallaj%C3%B6kull_eruption)
- IATA (press release, 21 April 2010): the crisis cost airlines "more than $1.7 billion in lost revenue" up to Tuesday 20 April. Losses peaked at "$400 million per day" from 17 to 19 April, partly offset by a fuel bill $110 million a day lower than normal. "At its worst, the crisis impacted 29% of global aviation and affected 1.2 million passengers a day" [primary, snippet] — [IATA press release, 21 April 2010](https://www.iata.org/en/pressroom/pressroom-archive/2010-press-releases/2010-04-21-01/); [Business Traveller](https://www.businesstraveller.com/news/carriers-lost-us17-billion-in-revenue-due-to-ash-fall/)
- Oxford Economics, for Airbus: the first week (15–21 April) cut world GDP by about US$4.7 billion, and about US$5.0 billion up to 24 May. The direct GDP effect was US$2,632 million in Europe, US$957 million in the Americas, US$591 million in the Middle East and Africa, and US$517 million in Asia. Aviation net losses were US$2.2 billion, lost visitor spending US$1.6 billion, and lost productivity from stranded workers US$490 million [commissioned consultancy report, via press, snippet] — [Australian Aviation](https://australianaviation.com.au/2010/05/ash-disruptions-cost-world-us5bn/); [CAPA](https://centreforaviation.com/news/ash-disruption-costs-usd47bn-in-global-gdp-airbus-50651)
- Kenya: the country normally exports up to 500 tonnes of flowers a day, 97 % of them to Europe. More than 3,000 tonnes of flowers were thrown away. The Kenya Flower Council put losses at about US$2 million a day (Capital FM; Mail & Guardian); a report of 18 April gave US$1.3 million a day (via Amandla); VOA gave about US$3 million a day for all horticulture. Farm workers were laid off (5,000 by 18 April in one report) [secondary press, 2010, snippet; these are estimates, not audited] — [Capital FM, "Kenya dumps tonnes of flowers"](https://www.capitalfm.co.ke/news/2010/04/kenya-dumps-tonnes-of-flowers/); [NPR Planet Money](https://www.npr.org/sections/money/2010/04/icelandic_volcano_hurts_kenyan.html); [VOA](https://www.voanews.com/amp/kenya-losing-millions-as-volcano-grounds-flights-91522474/154164.html); [Amandla, Kenya's farmers losing $1.3m a day](https://www.amandla.org.za/?p=415); [Christian Science Monitor](https://m.csmonitor.com/World/2010/0419/How-the-Iceland-volcano-ash-cloud-is-crippling-Kenya-s-flower-industry); [IPS](https://www.ipsnews.net/2010/04/africa-fair-trade-farmersrsquo-exports-hit-by-volcanic-ash/)

**Responses and management**
- Met Office, London VAAC: the UK Met Office is the Volcanic Ash Advisory Centre for north-west Europe and issues advisories under international standards. It said its dispersion model output matched those of the Canadian and French VAACs and was supported by aircraft and balloon observations. Thresholds for how much ash engines can take were set by the industry and the regulator, not by the Met Office [primary statements via trade press, snippet] — [Met Office response to CAA announcement (CAPA)](https://centreforaviation.com/members/direct-news/met-office-response-to-caa-announcement-25361); [Met Office volcanic ash update (CAPA)](https://centreforaviation.com/members/direct-news/met-office-volcanic-ash-update-25194). The eruption needed new, more complex model runs, with research staff working shifts [primary presentation, snippet] — [Met Office presentation (UNIGE CERG-C)](https://www.unige.ch/sciences/terre/CERG-C/download_file/view/255/330)
- The rule change: "On 20 April, the aircraft and engine manufacturers determined that their aircraft and engines would tolerate operations in an ash density of 2x10⁻³ g/m³" (2 mg/m³). This applied with precautionary maintenance and avoidance of visible ash [primary-derived, snippet] — [CAA teleconferences log](https://WWW.CAA.CO.UK/media/td4caq0n/teleconferences-log.pdf); [University of Hertfordshire paper](https://uhra.herts.ac.uk/id/eprint/4938/1/907121.pdf). "From 17 May 2010 the limits used by the UK Civil Aviation Authority (CAA) are 4 mg/m³ for the no fly zone, 2 mg/m³ for the time-limited zone, and 0.2 mg/m³ for the enhanced procedures zone" [secondary forum summary, snippet] — [COV6 Ash Forum summary](https://theghub.org/resources/44/download/COV6-Ash-Forum-Summary.pdf); [Wikipedia: Volcanic ash and aviation safety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcanic_ash_and_aviation_safety)
- The CAA's current levels are low (above 0.2 up to 2.0 mg/m³), medium (above 2.0 up to 4.0) and high (4.0 or more). Airlines need a safety case accepted by the CAA to fly in medium areas [primary, snippet] — [CAA, "Managing ash in UK airspace"](https://www.caa.co.uk/safety-initiatives/safety-projects/volcanic-ash/managing-ash-in-uk-airspace/)
- EU and ICAO: EU transport ministers called for a crisis coordination cell. The Commission and Eurocontrol set up the European Aviation Crisis Coordination Cell (EACCC) on 19 May 2010, and EASA joined soon after. ICAO set up an International Volcanic Ash Task Force in June 2010, and ICAO Doc 9974, "Flight safety and volcanic ash", became the basis for EASA's rules. Airlines may now decide whether to fly in ash-contaminated areas, based on a safety risk assessment that the national authority accepts. An ICAO exercise on 13–14 April 2011 tested the new contingency plan, and the EACCC was activated again for Grímsvötn in 2011 [primary EU and Eurocontrol sources via secondary pages, snippet] — [ReliefWeb, "Volcanic ash disruption: one year on and crisis preparedness"](https://reliefweb.int/report/world/volcanic-ash-disruption-one-year-and-crisis-preparedness); [European Parliament Legislative Observatory](https://oeil.europarl.europa.eu/oeil/en/document-summary?id=1109565); [International Airport Review](https://www.internationalairportreview.com/news/5183/european-aviation-crisis-coordination-cell-activated); [ASIL Insights](https://asil.org/insights/volume-14-issue-21/)
- The European Commission said passengers were "not however entitled to additional financial compensation in an exceptional circumstance like this" [primary statement via trade press, snippet] — [CAPA](https://centreforaviation.com/news/iceland-volcanic-eruption-closes-northern-european-airspace-45294)
- Earlier repository notes hold a peer-reviewed figure set that differs from Eurocontrol: about 108,000 flights cancelled, 10.5 million passengers and airline losses above US$1.7 billion [primary, carried over and not re-checked] — [Budd et al., "A fiasco of volcanic proportions? Eyjafjallajökull and the closure of European airspace"](https://figshare.com/articles/journal_contribution/A_fiasco_of_volcanic_proportions_Eyjafjallaj_kull_and_the_closure_of_European_airspace/9449402)

### Inferences

## 6. Merapi, Central Java, Indonesia, October–November 2010 (LIC/MIC alternative)

### Takeaway
Merapi's 2010 eruption was its largest since 1872 (Surono et al. 2012). The alert rose to Level IV (Awas) at 06:00 on 25 October, and people within 10 km were evacuated. On 26 October, at about 17:02, a blast killed about 30 people at Kinahrejo, including the gatekeeper Mbah Maridjan, aged 83. The climax on 4–5 November was VEI 4, with a 17 km ash column. Pyroclastic flows ran 15.5 km down the Gendol valley after the exclusion zone had been widened to 20 km. Deaths are given as 346 to 386 (Surono et al.: "nearly 400"). BNPB counted 279,702 evacuees on 8 November and 296,596 on 18 November; at least a third of a million people were displaced in all. USGS and Surono et al. estimate that the forecasts saved 10,000–20,000 lives. More than 250 lahars followed up to March 2012, most of them down Kali Putih.

### Cited Findings
**Causes and warnings (monitoring by CVGHM/PVMBG and BPPTK)**
- The paper: Surono et al. (2012), "The 2010 explosive eruption of Java's Merapi volcano—A '100-year' event", *Journal of Volcanology and Geothermal Research* 241–242, 121–135, doi:10.1016/j.jvolgeores.2012.06.018. Its abstract says the eruptions were Merapi's "largest and most explosive" in more than a century. They displaced "at least a third of a million people" and claimed "nearly 400 lives". CVGHM issued forecasts of the size of each eruptive phase, which the authors estimate saved 10,000–20,000 lives. Total SO₂ was about 0.44 Tg [primary, peer-reviewed, snippet] — [USGS publication record 70193579](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70193579); [USGS publication page](https://www.usgs.gov/publications/2010-explosive-eruption-javas-merapi-volcano-a-100-year-event); [full-text PDF (McGill course copy)](https://www.eps.mcgill.ca/~courses/c567/Week%204%20-%20Deformation%201/Surono%20et%20al%202012,%20Merapi%202010%20eruption.pdf)
- USGS VDAP: a team was sent at the request of the President of Indonesia to support CVGHM. Satellite radar could "see through" clouds and helped forecast pyroclastic flows. People living within 10 km of the summit were evacuated on 25 October. The decision to widen the zone from 15 to 20 km is credited with saving 10,000 to 20,000 lives ("20,0000" is a typo on the page) [primary, snippet] — [USGS VDAP: Merapi](https://volcanoes.usgs.gov/vdap/merapi.html). One search summary said this page dates the widening to **4 November**, but a second summary could not find that date, so the date is unconfirmed.
- Alert steps: Level II on 20 September, Level III on 21 October and Level IV on 25 October 2010. The rises were based on volcano-tectonic and multiphase earthquakes, rockfalls and deformation. PVG (the volcanology centre) raised Merapi from Siaga to **Awas from 06:00 local time** [primary, ESDM ministry, snippet] — [ESDM, "Status G. ## 7. Hunga Tonga–Hunga Haʻapai, 15 January 2022 (finale, scene 30)

### Takeaway
A submarine caldera on the Tonga–Kermadec arc, about 62–65 km north (north-north-west) of Nukuʻalofa, exploded on 15 January 2022. Its plume reached about 57 km, the first observed injection straight into the mesosphere (Proud et al. 2022, *Science*). Its air-pressure Lamb wave went round the Earth four times (plus three antipodal passes) in six days, as large as Krakatau's in 1883. NASA's preliminary estimate of the energy was 4–18 Mt TNT, revised from 5–30 Mt. Tsunami waves reached about 15–17 m on Tongatapu. Three deaths in Tonga were confirmed early, and two people drowned in Peru. The international cable was cut in two places, and Tonga was largely offline for more than five weeks, until 22 February. The plume added about 146 Tg of water to the stratosphere, about 10 % of what is normally there. The World Bank estimated damage at US$90.4 million, about 18.5 % of GDP.

### Cited Findings
- Location: about 62 km NNW of Nukuʻalofa (the GVP figure as quoted by Wikipedia) or 62 km "northwest" (Atlas Obscura). Other sources give 64–65 km north. It lies on the Tonga–Kermadec arc, where the Pacific Plate is subducted westward at the Tonga Trench [secondary, snippet] — [Wikipedia: 2009 Tonga undersea eruption (quoting GVP)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2009_Tonga_undersea_volcanic_eruption); [Atlas Obscura](https://www.atlasobscura.com/places/hunga-ha-apai-new-volcano-island); [Al Jazeera explainer](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2022/1/16/explainer-what-does-volcanic-eruption-mean-for-tonga-world)
- Lead-up: an eruptive episode began on 20 December 2021 and paused on 5 January 2022, before the large events of 14–15 January. The GVP weekly report for 16–22 February 2022 covered cable repairs and the return of internet service [primary, snippet] — [GVP Hunga Tonga-Hunga Ha'apai](https://volcano.si.edu/volcano.cfm?vn=243040); [GVP Bulletin report, February 2022 (via ReliefWeb)](https://m.reliefweb.int/report/3839515/tonga/report-hunga-tonga-hunga-haapai-tonga-february-2022?lang=es)
- Plume: "the volcanic plume reached an altitude of 57 kilometers at its highest extent. This places the plume in the lower mesosphere." It is the first observational evidence of material injected through the stratosphere directly into the mesosphere, and heights of past plumes such as Pinatubo 1991 (recorded at about 40 km) may have been underestimated [primary, peer-reviewed, snippet] — [Proud, Prata and Schmauß 2022, *Science* 378, 554–557, doi:10.1126/science.abo4076 (Monash record)](https://research.monash.edu/en/publications/the-january-2022-eruption-of-hunga-tonga-hunga-haapai-volcano-rea/); [University of Oxford news](https://www.ox.ac.uk/node/7655056); [RAL Space](https://www.ralspace.stfc.ac.uk/Pages/Tonga-volcano-had-highest-plume-ever-recorded.aspx)
- Pressure wave: "the surface-guided Lamb wave (≲0.01 hertz), which we observed propagating for four (plus three antipodal) passages around Earth over 6 days". As measured by Lamb-wave amplitude, the climactic explosion "was comparable in size to that of the 1883 Krakatau eruption". The eruption also caused audible sound about 10,000 km away and disturbances in the ionosphere [primary, peer-reviewed, snippet] — [Matoza et al. 2022, *Science*, "Atmospheric waves and global seismoacoustic observations of the January 2022 Hunga eruption, Tonga" (open copy, University of Florence)](https://flore.unifi.it/bitstream/2158/1284463/1/science.abo7063.pdf); [University of Reading eprint](https://reading-clone.eprints-hosting.org/105147)
- Energy: NASA's Jim Garvin first gave 5 to 30 Mt of TNT, then a preliminary 4 to 18 Mt, "hundreds of times" the Hiroshima bomb (about 15 kt). The estimate was based on how much rock was removed, how resistant it was and how high the cloud went [secondary press quoting NASA, snippet] — [Live Science](https://livescience.com/tonga-volcano-hiroshima-bomb); [Inquirer, quoting AFP and NASA](https://newsinfo.inquirer.net/1544842/tonga-eruption-equivalent-to-hundreds-of-hiroshima-nasa/amp)
- Water vapour: about 146 Tg (1 Tg = 10¹² g) went into the stratosphere, about 10 % of the water vapour normally there and the largest such injection since measurements began in 1995. Press reports also say it was about four times Pinatubo's [primary paper via press and a citing NOAA paper, snippet] — [Schoeberl et al. 2022 (NOAA repository), citing Millán et al. 2022, *GRL*](https://repository.library.noaa.gov/view/noaa/54594/noaa_54594_DS1.pdf); [EarthSky](https://earthsky.org/earth/tonga-eruption-weaken-ozone-layer/)
- Caldera: the eruption destroyed most of the two visible islands and left a hole about 4 km wide and 1 km deep [secondary teachers' association, snippet] — [NZASE science update, February 2023](https://nzase.org.nz/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/2023-02-Tongan-eruption-science-update-NZASE-news-article.pdf). For volumes see Q4.
- Tsunami heights: a model estimated a maximum runup of about 15 m on the north-west coast of Tongatapu within 10 minutes, with waves covering the whole island within 30 minutes. A calibrated model gives up to 17 m on Tongatapu and up to 45 m on Tofua [primary, peer-reviewed, snippet; the summary attributed the 15 m figure to Pakoksung, Suppasri and Imamura 2022 (*Scientific Reports*) and the 17/45 m figures to Purkis et al. 2023 (*Science Advances*); match each to the PMC records below before citing] — [PMC9452557, "The near-field tsunami generated by the 15 January 2022 eruption… and its impact on Tongatapu"](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9452557/); [PMC10104470](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10104470). The field survey (Borrero et al. 2022, March–June 2022) measured runup on Tongatapu, ʻEua and the Haʻapai group — [Borrero et al. 2022 (PDF)](https://data-ww3.ifremer.fr/BIB/Borrero_etal_PAG2022.pdf)
- Deaths in Tonga: the government's first update confirmed three deaths. They were Angela Glover, a 50-year-old British woman; a 65-year-old woman on Mango; and a 49-year-old man on Nomuka. Every home on Mango (about 50 residents) was destroyed. The ash plume covered all of Tonga's roughly 170 islands, home to more than 100,000 people [secondary press, January 2022, snippet] — [PBS NewsHour, "Three dead in volcano eruption aftermath"](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/world/three-dead-in-volcano-eruption-aftermath-but-tonga-appears-to-avoid-major-destruction); [Newstalk ZB, "Tonga death toll: three dead, UN says"](https://www.newstalkzb.co.nz/news/world/tonga-death-toll-three-dead-after-eruption-and-tsunami-un-says); [PINA, 20 January 2022](https://pina.com.fj/2022/01/20/tonga-says-volcanic-eruption-and-tsunami-an-unprecedented-disaster/)
- Peru: two people drowned on a beach in Lambayeque, northern Peru, confirmed by Indeci, the civil defence institute. They were two women at Naylamp beach (the second-hand detail comes from the Daily Mail via the NZ Herald). More than 20 Peruvian ports were closed as a precaution [secondary press citing Reuters and Indeci, snippet] — [Newstalk ZB / Reuters](https://www.newstalkzb.co.nz/news/world/tonga-eruption-tsunami-two-people-in-peru-drown-as-surge-hits)
- La Pampilla oil spill: waves struck a tanker unloading at Repsol's La Pampilla refinery north of Lima, and 264,000 gallons of crude oil were spilled. The president declared an environmental emergency on 20 January 2022. The investigation into Repsol's role was still under way at the time of reporting [secondary, snippet] — [Smithsonian Magazine](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/peru-declares-environmental-emergency-after-devastating-oil-spill-coats-the-coast-180979456/)

Tonga used wireless links in the meantime, and Starlink was tested for the outer islands [secondary press citing Tonga Cable Ltd, Digicel and AP, snippet] — [KUTV / AP, "Tonga's internet finally restored 5 weeks after big eruption"](https://kutv.com/news/nation-world/tongas-internet-finally-restored-5-weeks-after-big-eruption); [The Register, 22 February 2022](https://www.theregister.com/2022/02/22/tonga_cable_reconnected/); [AIIA Australian Outlook](https://www.internationalaffairs.org.au/australianoutlook/volcano-cuts-communication-island-kingdom/)

Wider losses, such as continuing effects on agriculture and tourism, were not included [primary, snippet] — [World Bank press release, 14 February 2022](https://www.worldbank.org/en/news/press-release/2022/02/14/tonga-volcanic-eruption-and-tsunami-world-bank-disaster-assessment-report-estimates-damages-at-us-90m)
